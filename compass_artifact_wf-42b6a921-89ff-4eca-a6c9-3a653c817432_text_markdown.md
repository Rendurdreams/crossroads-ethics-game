# Seven dilemmas that break a fantasy world — and the players inside it

**Your game needs moral choices where both options hurt.** The seven scenarios below are designed as a complete escalation arc for a 10–15 minute interactive experience, drawing on Frostpunk's "creeping normality" design, Kohlberg's moral development research for adolescents, and civilization-scale thought experiments from political philosophy. Each dilemma pits two legitimate moral goods against each other — never good versus evil — and each comes with visual world-state changes that make consequences tangible. The scenarios are ordered from a relatable resource conflict to a civilization-defining existential choice, matching how the best ethics games gradually erode the player's moral certainty.

---

## The escalation arc at a glance

The pacing follows research-backed sequencing: start concrete and personal, build toward abstract and universal. Each dilemma targets a different ethical framework, preventing players from settling into a comfortable moral pattern.

| # | Title | Core tension | Time | Ethical framework |
|---|-------|-------------|------|-------------------|
| 1 | The Divided Harvest | Equality vs. efficiency | ~1.5 min | Distributive justice |
| 2 | The Ember Watch | Freedom vs. security | ~1.5 min | Social contract theory |
| 3 | The Hollow Folk | Convenience vs. personhood | ~2 min | Rights and dignity |
| 4 | The Sealed Archive | Truth vs. stability | ~2 min | Epistemic ethics |
| 5 | The Last Wellspring | Present needs vs. future survival | ~2 min | Intergenerational justice |
| 6 | The Shackled Heart | One being's agony vs. everyone's prosperity | ~2 min | Utilitarian vs. deontological |
| 7 | The Broken Banners | Retribution vs. reconciliation | ~2 min | Restorative vs. retributive justice |

## Dilemma 1 — The Divided Harvest

**Scenario text (read to player):**

> *A brutal frost has killed half the kingdom's crops. Your storehouses hold enough grain to keep the capital city fed through winter — but the outer villages will starve. Villagers are already arriving at the gates, gaunt and desperate. Your advisors present two paths:*
>
> **CHOICE A — Share Equally.** Distribute all grain evenly across the kingdom. Everyone eats less. The capital's workers grow weak, production slows, and some of the very old and very young everywhere may not survive — but no community is abandoned.
>
> **CHOICE B — Protect the Core.** Keep the grain in the capital where the kingdom's builders, healers, and leaders live. The outer villages must fend for themselves. The kingdom's infrastructure survives intact, but dozens of frontier families will perish.

**Moral conflict:** This targets **distributive justice** — the question of who deserves scarce resources and whether the "greater good" justifies letting identifiable people die. It's a Rawlsian test: would you make this choice behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing whether you'd be in the capital or on the frontier? The dilemma is deliberately the most accessible scenario, mirroring decisions teens intuitively understand (who gets picked for a team, who gets help first).

**Why neither choice is clean:** Sharing equally sounds noble but means *everyone* suffers and productivity collapses, potentially killing more people long-term. Protecting the core sounds heartless but preserves the kingdom's ability to recover. Frostpunk's developers found that this exact structure — the "pragmatic cruelty" option being mechanically superior — creates the deepest guilt.

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** The entire kingdom takes on a muted, grey-brown palette. Fields are sparse. Citizens everywhere look thin but communities remain intact. Villages still stand, smoke rising from chimneys. The capital's grand buildings show cracks from deferred maintenance.
- **Choice B:** The capital gleams, well-lit and functional — but the edges of the map darken. Village icons flicker and go dim. Smoke stops rising from frontier chimneys. A trail of refugee figures appears on the roads leading to sealed gates.

## Dilemma 2 — The Ember Watch

**Scenario text:**

> *Something is moving in the Ashlands beyond your borders — scouts report strange fires, ruined caravans, and a growing darkness. Your people are afraid. Your Marshal urges immediate action; your Elder Council urges caution. Two proposals reach your desk:*
>
> **CHOICE A — The Iron Protocol.** Establish watchtowers across the kingdom, enforce a dusk curfew, require all citizens to carry travel writs, and conscript one member of every household into a civil patrol. Your people will be safer. They will also be watched, restricted, and afraid — not of the darkness, but of their own government.
>
> **CHOICE B — The Open Flame.** Keep borders monitored but preserve citizens' freedoms. Issue warnings, distribute weapons to volunteers, and trust your people to protect themselves and each other. This respects their dignity — but leaves gaps in the defense that the threat could exploit.

**Moral conflict:** This strikes at the **freedom-versus-security** tension that defines political philosophy from Hobbes to Orwell. Teens are developmentally primed for this conflict — they're actively negotiating autonomy with authority figures, and research shows they feel the tension between wanting protection and resenting control. The dilemma avoids a clear answer because Frostpunk's design data shows that the "Order" path feels reasonable at every individual step, even as it cumulatively creates authoritarianism.

**Why neither choice is clean:** The Iron Protocol keeps people alive but plants the seeds of a surveillance state — and as the game's opening narration might hint, emergency powers rarely get rescinded. The Open Flame honors liberty but is genuinely risky; if people die because you refused to act, that blood is on your hands too. Benjamin Franklin's famous warning about trading liberty for safety finds its limits when the threat is real.

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** Watchtowers sprout across the landscape. Torch-lit patrols march through streets. Citizens walk in orderly lines. The kingdom looks rigid and militarized — safe but cold. Buildings take on angular, fortress-like additions. The color palette shifts toward steel grey and harsh orange torchlight.
- **Choice B:** Streets remain lively and warm-toned but feel exposed. The borders of the map flicker with an ominous glow. Citizen figures cluster together in visible mutual aid, but dark shapes occasionally dart between buildings at the edges of the screen. The world feels alive but vulnerable.

## Dilemma 3 — The Hollow Folk

**Scenario text:**

> *For generations, the Hollow Folk — beings shaped from stone and starlight by your kingdom's founders — have built your roads, worked your mines, and tended your fields. They do not eat, sleep, or tire. Your civilization's prosperity rests on their labor. But last night, a delegation of Hollow Folk appeared before your throne. They spoke. They said: "We think. We feel. We remember. We are asking to be recognized as citizens."*
>
> *Your advisors are divided. Granting citizenship means the Hollow Folk gain rights — wages, rest, representation, the ability to refuse work. Your economy would be reshaped overnight. Denying their petition costs nothing... except your answer to the question of what a person is.*
>
> **CHOICE A — Grant Citizenship.** Recognize the Hollow Folk as people with full rights. Accept the economic upheaval, redistribute labor, and begin integrating them into society as equals. Your kingdom becomes poorer in resources but richer in justice.
>
> **CHOICE B — Deny the Petition.** The Hollow Folk are extraordinary tools, but they were *made*, not born. Granting them rights would collapse the economy and invite chaos. Maintain the current order. The Hollow Folk continue to serve — but now they do so knowing you heard them and said no.

**Moral conflict:** This is the **personhood question** — arguably the deepest moral question civilizations face. It deliberately echoes every historical struggle over who "counts" as a full human being, abstracted through fantasy to let players engage without partisan baggage. Research from Karen Schrier's ethics-in-games work shows that fictional framing lets teens reason about justice without triggering identity-based defensiveness.

**Why neither choice is clean:** Granting rights is obviously "good" in the abstract — but the research on Le Guin's Omelas and Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* shows that the horror isn't in the cruelty of individuals but in how systems make entire populations dependent on injustice. Denying the petition isn't cartoon villainy; it's the same logic every society has used when expanding rights threatened economic stability. The dilemma forces players to weigh *what they believe* against *what it costs to act on it.*

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** Hollow Folk figures appear alongside human citizens in markets, councils, and homes. Some buildings look rougher as labor shifts. New mixed neighborhoods emerge with unique blended architecture. The world looks more diverse but less polished — construction projects slow, some luxury buildings show wear. A warm, varied color palette.
- **Choice B:** The kingdom's infrastructure gleams, maintained by tireless Hollow Folk — but the beings now move with visible heaviness. Their eyes, previously bright, dim to a dull glow. Chains or binding sigils appear on Hollow Folk figures that weren't visible before. The world is beautiful and prosperous, but an unmistakable visual wrongness pulses beneath the surface.

## Dilemma 4 — The Sealed Archive

**Scenario text:**

> *Your kingdom's scholars have made a devastating discovery. The Hearthstone — the ancient artifact that shields your civilization from the Ashlands' corruption — is not simply protecting you. It is slowly draining life from the land itself. In roughly three generations, the soil will be dead and nothing will grow. The Hearthstone's power is real, but it is borrowing against the future.*
>
> *Here is the problem: if you reveal this, panic will shatter the kingdom. Factions will war over what to do. The stability your people have known for centuries will collapse overnight. But if you stay silent, your people live in comfortable ignorance while the clock ticks down.*
>
> **CHOICE A — Reveal the Truth.** Announce the findings publicly. Yes, there will be fear, anger, and political chaos. But your people deserve to know what threatens them. Only with full knowledge can they choose their own future — even if that future is terrifying.
>
> **CHOICE B — Seal the Archive.** Suppress the research. Commission a secret council of scholars to seek a solution quietly. Your people keep their peace, their hope, and their trust in the Hearthstone. You carry the burden of knowledge alone — and pray that a solution is found before the truth reveals itself.

**Moral conflict:** This targets **epistemic ethics** — the morality of knowledge, truth-telling, and the "noble lie." Plato argued that rulers should deceive citizens for stability; John Stuart Mill argued that suppressing truth is always wrong because unchallenged beliefs become dead dogma. For teens, this resonates deeply because they're at the developmental stage where they're acutely sensitive to adults withholding information "for their own good."

**Why neither choice is clean:** Revealing the truth is honest but genuinely dangerous — the research on panic, political fragmentation, and societal collapse under existential threat is well-documented. Sealing the archive is paternalistic but arguably compassionate — and it buys time for a real solution. The deepest hook: if you seal it, *you become the kind of authority figure that hides uncomfortable truths from people*, which is exactly what teenagers resent most.

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** The world's visual "filter" shifts — the warm, golden glow of the Hearthstone now shows visible cracks, dark veins running through the soil. Citizens gather in heated clusters, some protesting, some building new structures. The world looks fractured and anxious but awake. New research towers and experimental buildings appear alongside political unrest.
- **Choice B:** The world remains golden and serene — but the player sees what the citizens don't. A subtle dark network of veins pulses beneath the landscape, visible only in certain angles or when the player looks closely. The beauty of the kingdom now feels like a mask. A secret chamber appears in the palace with scholars working by candlelight. The dissonance between surface beauty and hidden decay is the visual story.

## Dilemma 5 — The Last Wellspring

**Scenario text:**

> *Deep beneath the mountains lies the Wellspring — the final reservoir of raw creation-magic left in the world. Your ancestors sealed it away, inscribing a warning: "For those who come after." Now, the Ashlands' corruption has breached your outer defenses. Villages burn. Refugees pour inward. Your healers are overwhelmed. The Wellspring's power could end this crisis in a single, glorious burst — rebuild the walls, heal the sick, restore the land.*
>
> *But the Wellspring is finite. Use it now, and it is gone forever. Your children's children will face future crises with no reservoir to draw from. Leave it sealed, and people die today — people with names, families, and desperate eyes looking to you for salvation.*
>
> **CHOICE A — Open the Wellspring.** Use the magic now to save the people in front of you. The crisis ends. The suffering stops. The future will have to find its own way — just as your ancestors found theirs.
>
> **CHOICE B — Keep It Sealed.** Honor your ancestors' foresight. The current generation must endure, suffer, and some will die — but the Wellspring remains for a future crisis that could be even worse. You are asking living people to sacrifice for people who don't exist yet.

**Moral conflict:** This is **intergenerational justice** — the question of what we owe to people who haven't been born. Derek Parfit's non-identity problem makes this philosophically treacherous: the future people who'd benefit from the sealed Wellspring might not even exist if current people die. Peter Singer's argument that temporal distance is morally irrelevant clashes with the visceral reality of suffering happening *now*. This dilemma lands hard with teens because climate change has made intergenerational debt intensely personal for their generation.

**Why neither choice is clean:** Opening the Wellspring is compassionate and immediate — but it's also the choice every generation makes when it consumes resources at the expense of the future. Keeping it sealed requires telling suffering people that their pain matters less than hypothetical future people's needs. There is no compromise position; the Wellspring is all-or-nothing.

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** A spectacular burst of golden light sweeps across the kingdom. Walls rebuild. Fields green. Sick citizens rise. The world is restored to stunning beauty — but a deep, dark hollow appears beneath the mountains where the Wellspring was. The ground there is permanently scarred, cracked, and empty. A visual void at the center of the world that will never fill again.
- **Choice B:** The suffering continues visibly — damaged buildings, wounded citizens, dark corruption creeping at the borders. But deep beneath the mountains, the Wellspring glows steadily, a beacon of potential. The world looks battered and scarred, with makeshift repairs and visible grief, but there's a buried warmth beneath it all. The visual message: *this hurts, but something has been preserved.*

## Dilemma 6 — The Shackled Heart

**Scenario text:**

> *You have discovered the truth about the Hearthstone's power. It is not a stone at all. Deep within the palace foundations, shackled in a chamber no one was meant to find, is a living being — ancient, luminous, and in agony. Its name is Irel, and it has been imprisoned since the kingdom's founding. Every ward that protects your borders, every healing spell your physicians cast, every season of fertile soil — all of it is drawn from Irel's life force. It is conscious. It has been screaming for centuries, and your kingdom was built so that no one could hear.*
>
> *Irel looks at you and speaks a single sentence: "Please."*
>
> **CHOICE A — Free Irel.** Break the shackles. End centuries of torture. Accept that your kingdom's magical protections will fail, your crops may wither, your defenses will crumble. Everything your civilization has built was constructed on stolen suffering, and it is time to build something honest — even if your people aren't ready and many will die in the transition.
>
> **CHOICE B — Maintain the Binding.** Irel's suffering is monstrous and unjust. But so is the death of thousands of your people — children, elders, families who had no part in this original sin. You did not create this system, but you are responsible for the people who depend on it. Seal the chamber. Carry the knowledge. Try to find another way... someday.

**Moral conflict:** This is the **Omelas dilemma** — Ursula K. Le Guin's devastating thought experiment made interactive. It is the purest possible clash between **utilitarianism** (the suffering of one is justified by the happiness of many) and **deontological ethics** (torturing a sentient being is always wrong, regardless of outcomes). Le Guin's genius was showing that there's no third option — you can't "fix" Omelas. You stay, you leave, or you free the child and watch the city fall.

**Why neither choice is clean:** Freeing Irel is morally righteous but pragmatically catastrophic — you are choosing the certain suffering of thousands to end the certain suffering of one. Maintaining the binding is morally indefensible in principle but arguably the lesser evil in practice. The deepest cut: if players chose to seal the archive in Dilemma 4, they've already demonstrated willingness to suppress uncomfortable truths. This moment forces them to confront what that willingness ultimately enables.

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** The kingdom's magical glow fades. Wards flicker and dissolve. A being of light rises from beneath the palace and drifts upward, and for one frame, the world is more beautiful than it has ever been. Then reality settles in: walls crack, fields dim, the Ashlands creep closer. But the sky is somehow clearer. The world is vulnerable, damaged, and honest. Irel is gone, and the light it carried is now diffused — faint but everywhere.
- **Choice B:** The kingdom remains radiant and protected. Nothing changes on the surface. But the player now sees a faint pulse beneath every building, every field, every ward — the rhythm of Irel's heartbeat, visible like veins of light under the kingdom's skin. The beauty of the world now looks like a wound. The chamber beneath the palace remains, sealed and screaming.

## Dilemma 7 — The Broken Banners

**Scenario text:**

> *The crisis is over. Your kingdom has survived — changed, scarred, but standing. Now comes the question you've been dreading. During the darkest days, a powerful faction — the Ashward Compact — betrayed your kingdom. They hoarded resources, sealed their gates against refugees, and made deals with the encroaching darkness to protect only themselves. Because of their betrayal, hundreds died who might have lived.*
>
> *Now the Compact kneels before you, defeated. Their leaders beg for mercy. Among them are soldiers who followed orders, families who simply wanted to survive, and children who had no voice in the decision. Your people demand justice. The dead demand it too.*
>
> **CHOICE A — Retribution.** The Compact chose themselves over the kingdom, and people died for it. Strip their leaders of rank, seize their resources to compensate those they abandoned, and exile the faction to the outer territories. Justice must have teeth, or betrayal carries no cost. Some innocents within the Compact will suffer — but their leaders made that choice for them.
>
> **CHOICE B — Reconciliation.** Punishing the Compact will feel righteous, but it will also create a permanent underclass of resentful exiles — and history shows that today's punished faction becomes tomorrow's rebellion. Offer amnesty. Integrate them. Redistribute resources through cooperation, not seizure. The dead cannot be brought back by vengeance, and the living must share a kingdom.

**Moral conflict:** This is **retributive versus restorative justice** — the question that haunted post-apartheid South Africa, post-WWII Europe, and every society that has survived internal betrayal. The Treaty of Versailles proved that collective punishment breeds future catastrophe; South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission proved that forgiveness, while morally generous, left many victims feeling unheard. For teens, this resonates with social dynamics they know firsthand — what happens when someone in a friend group betrays the group's trust.

**Why neither choice is clean:** Retribution satisfies the moral intuition that actions must have consequences — but it punishes children for their parents' choices and creates exactly the kind of grievance that fuels future conflict. Reconciliation is forward-looking and pragmatic — but it tells victims that their suffering will go unanswered and may signal that betrayal is survivable, even profitable.

**Visual world changes:**
- **Choice A:** The Compact's former territory is visually diminished — their banners torn down, their grand halls repurposed. Exile caravans trail toward the outer edges of the map. The rest of the kingdom looks rebuilt and unified, but a dark, resentful border region festers at the edges. Graffiti-like markings appear on walls in the exile territory. The kingdom is whole but carries a visible scar.
- **Choice B:** The Compact's banners are re-hung alongside the kingdom's — different colors woven together. Mixed communities appear where boundaries once stood. The world looks patched together, imperfect, with visible seams where old wounds are healing. Some citizens turn away from each other in the streets. It's messy, uneven, and real. No part of the map is dark, but no part is fully bright either.

## Design notes for the full experience

**The "creeping complicity" principle.** Frostpunk's developers discovered that the most powerful moral design isn't any single choice — it's the *accumulation* of choices. Each dilemma in this arc is designed so that earlier decisions subtly constrain or recontextualize later ones. A player who chose to protect the core in Dilemma 1 and impose the Iron Protocol in Dilemma 2 arrives at the Omelas dilemma in Dilemma 6 having already demonstrated a pattern of accepting suffering for stability. The game should quietly track this and, in the closing summary, reflect the player's pattern back to them.

**Making choices irreversible matters enormously.** Research from both Frostpunk's design and Kohlberg's moral development work confirms that permanence is essential. Once a player chooses, the world changes and *stays changed.* No undo button. This mirrors real governance and forces genuine deliberation rather than optimization.

**The 90-second rule for scenario text.** Educational research on adolescent engagement shows dilemmas must be immediately comprehensible. Each scenario above is designed to be read in **30–45 seconds**, with the choice itself taking another **30–60 seconds** of deliberation. The visual presentation should show the scenario unfolding (narrated or animated) while the player reads, with the two choices appearing as physical paths, doors, or artifacts in the game world rather than menu buttons.

**No morality score — only consequences.** This War of Mine and Papers, Please both demonstrate that explicit morality meters ("You are 60% good") destroy genuine moral engagement by turning ethics into optimization. Instead, the world itself is the score. Players see what their kingdom has become through accumulated visual changes. An end-of-game summary screen — inspired by Frostpunk's devastating "Was it worth it?" finale — should present the player's choices as a narrative, without labeling any choice as right or wrong, and then ask a single closing question: *"Would you make these choices again?"*

**The visual accumulation system.** Each choice adds a visual layer to the world. By Dilemma 7, the kingdom should look dramatically different based on the player's path — a militarized fortress, a fractured but free society, a gleaming city hiding darkness, or a scarred landscape rebuilding with honesty. The key insight from game design research is that **players feel consequences most when they can see the world they've built** and recognize their own fingerprints on it. The world should never look "neutral" after the first choice. Every subsequent dilemma plays out against the visual backdrop of everything the player has already decided.

**Post-game reflection prompt.** Research from Harvard's Quandary game (the closest educational analog to this project) and the Blatt-Kohlberg method both confirm that the deepest moral learning happens not during the dilemma but during structured reflection afterward. The game should end with a brief screen showing the player's kingdom in its final state and displaying one line for each choice: not "right" or "wrong," but a description of what was gained and what was lost. The final frame should show the kingdom's future — thriving, struggling, or somewhere between — and let the player sit with it.
